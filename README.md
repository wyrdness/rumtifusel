## Overview

The Rumtifusel (also spelled Rumptifusel) belongs to the corpus of fearsome critters — humorous tall-tale beasts told in North American logging camps in the late 19th and early 20th centuries and catalogued in print by Henry H. Tryon in 'Fearsome Critters' (1939). It is described as large, flat, very flexible, and covered in a fine pelt resembling mink. The Rumtifusel hunts by mimicry: it drapes itself across a tree stump or fallen log so it looks like an expensive abandoned fur coat. When a 'tenderfoot' or other greedy passer-by approaches to claim the coat, the Rumtifusel — moving with deceptive speed — wraps around its victim. Its underside is lined with tiny suction pores that drain the victim until only bones remain. As with the rest of the fearsome-critter canon, the Rumtifusel is a humorous folkloric construct used for storytelling and the hazing of new lumberjacks rather than a claimed zoological animal.
